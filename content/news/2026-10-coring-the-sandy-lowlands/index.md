Much of what we know about past landscapes is buried just beneath our feet. In the sandy lowlands of Flanders, old river channels and hollows slowly filled with peat and organic mud as the Ice Age came to an end. Layer by layer, they trapped pollen, seeds and traces of DNA from the plants growing around them.

## Getting the sediment out

To reach those layers, we take **cores**: long, continuous tubes of sediment. In soft deposits we use a hand-operated Russian peat corer, which cuts a half-cylinder of sediment without squashing it. For deeper sequences, a specialist firm drills mechanical cores.

Back in the lab, every core is described, photographed and sampled at close intervals for grain size, magnetic susceptibility, organic content, pollen and sedimentary DNA. Samples for DNA are handled with particular care to avoid contamination, and frozen immediately.

## Putting dates on the layers

The key to turning a core into a history is dating. We pick out tiny remains of short-lived plants, such as seeds, for radiocarbon dating, and combine the dates in a statistical age–depth model. The result is a timeline for each core, so that we can see exactly when the landscape changed as the Younger Dryas began and ended.
