Around 14,600 years ago, the last Ice Age was loosening its grip on Europe. Temperatures rose, birch and pine woodland spread north, and hunter-gatherers followed, moving back into regions that had been largely empty for thousands of years.

Then, around **12,850 years ago**, the warming went into reverse. Within a short time, north-west Europe was plunged back into near-glacial cold. This cold phase, the **Younger Dryas**, lasted roughly 1,200 years before the climate warmed again at the start of the present interglacial, the Holocene, around 11,650 years ago.

## Why "Dryas"?

The name comes from *Dryas octopetala*, the mountain avens, a small white-flowered plant of cold, open ground. Its leaves turn up in lake sediments from this period across northern Europe, a sign that tundra-like vegetation had returned. "Younger" distinguishes it from earlier cold phases also marked by the plant.

## What caused it?

The leading explanation is a surge of fresh meltwater from the North American and Scandinavian ice sheets into the North Atlantic. Fresh water is lighter than salt water, so it can slow the ocean circulation that carries warm water northwards towards Europe. With that heat supply reduced, Europe cooled sharply.

## What did it mean for people?

Woodland retreated, resources that hunter-gatherers relied on became scarcer, and large reindeer herds returned to the plains. Across much of Western Europe, traces of human activity become far rarer in this period.
